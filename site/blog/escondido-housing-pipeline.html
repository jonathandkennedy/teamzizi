What is actually being built in Escondido &mdash; and what the state is demanding

Written by Zohra Azizi, CA DRE# 01992847 · Updated 2026-07-30

A wave of downtown approvals is remaking Valley Parkway block by block while the state formally questions the city’s housing-element compliance. The projects with real approvals, the scoreboard against the 9,607-unit mandate, and the one big project that is going nowhere. Revised quarterly.

What housing is actually approved or under construction in Escondido right now?

Escondido’s pipeline concentrates on and around downtown’s Valley Parkway: The Maple, 128 apartments in five stories across from City Hall, approved January 2026; Valley Parkway Townhomes, 94 for-sale homes approved unanimously in June 2026; a 70-townhome KB Home project by the transit center approved December 2025; and Quince Street Senior Apartments, 145 affordable senior homes already under construction across from the transit center.

Behind those sit the wave’s two largest pieces: Palomar Heights, the 510-home redevelopment of the former hospital site approved in 2021, and the county’s Valley Creek project at 620 E. Valley Parkway — 134 affordable senior homes plus a childcare facility on surplus county land, in environmental review as of May 2026 with construction expected in 2028. The pattern is consistent: density is going downtown, near transit, on already-developed land.

Is Escondido on pace for its state housing mandate?

Escondido’s state-assigned target for the 2021–2029 cycle is 9,607 homes, and the city’s own annual progress reporting puts permits at roughly 2,300 through 2025 — about a quarter of the mandate with the cycle past half-run, and heavily tilted toward market-rate homes.

The tilt is the part regulators watch: as reported by Voice of San Diego from the city’s 2025 figures, roughly 70% of that year’s 369 permits were above-moderate-income homes, and the affordable categories sit far behind — on the order of 11% of the very-low-income target and 6% of moderate. Escondido is not unusual in lagging, but the gap is what gives the state’s December letter its teeth.

Why is the state questioning Escondido's housing plan?

On December 3, 2025, the state housing department sent Escondido a formal letter of inquiry listing eight overdue housing-element programs — from an unadopted ADU ordinance to the never-established affordable-housing trust fund — and warning it may revoke the city’s compliance finding, an outcome that would expose the city to the builder’s remedy, under which qualifying projects can bypass local zoning.

The first visible response landed in June 2026: a unanimous overhaul of the city’s ADU rules making approval ministerial and allowing separate ADU sale under state law. The harder item is structural — the letter formally identifies Proposition S, the 1998 measure requiring voter approval for General Plan density increases, as a constraint the city must mitigate. That collision between a voter-approved measure and a state mandate is the live storyline of Escondido land-use politics.

Is any of the new Escondido housing for sale, or is it all apartments?

The 2025–26 approvals mark a shift toward ownership product in Escondido: 94 for-sale townhomes on West Valley Parkway and 70 by the transit center were approved within seven months, and the council has been explicit about wanting “missing middle” homes — the duplex-to-cottage-court range — as an ownership on-ramp.

The June 2026 approval came with a developer expectation, per The Coast News, of pricing from the low $600,000s — a dated, attributed figure that will move, but a marker of what new for-sale product downtown looks like. For buyers priced against the newer master-planned inventory elsewhere in North County, new construction without Mello-Roos is a combination worth understanding — the Escondido decision post covers the tax structure.

What happened to Harvest Hills (Safari Highlands Ranch)?

Harvest Hills — the proposed 550-home annexation on 1,098 unincorporated acres on Escondido’s eastern edge, east of Rancho San Pasqual — remains where it has been for years: application under review, no council vote ever scheduled, and no hearing on any posted agenda as of July 2026.

Its environmental review ran back in 2017, and a council vote once expected in 2020 never happened. Buyers near the eastern edges sometimes hear the project cited as imminent in both directions — as a threat and as a promise. The checkable fact is narrower: the city’s own project page lists the application as pending with staff, nothing more. Treat any claim beyond that as unpublished until an agenda says otherwise.

How do you track what gets built next in Escondido?

Escondido’s development record lives in three public places: the City Council and Planning Commission agendas, the city’s project pages for named developments, and the annual housing progress report each spring — the same sources this page is built from.

This post is revised quarterly as approvals land or stall. For the community-level picture — the four ZIP codes, the two school districts, what the tax line looks like — start with the Escondido guide and the decision post.

Project facts above are from City of Escondido hearing notices and council records, the California HCD letter of December 3, 2025, and named local reporting (The Coast News, Voice of San Diego), as of the dates cited. Approvals are not completions; construction status changes. The city’s agendas are the record, and this page is revised quarterly.
